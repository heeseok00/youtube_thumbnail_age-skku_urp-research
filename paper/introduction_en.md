# 1. Introduction

YouTube is now a main digital space for news, health, education, and everyday information, not only for entertainment. Older adults also use online video for practical knowledge and lifelong learning. Through interviews and large-scale viewing logs from K-MOOC lecture videos, Kim et al. (2023) found that older adults learn material they can apply in daily life and that their viewing practices and difficulties differ from those of younger users. Once several generations share one platform, it matters how content is shown and worded for different age groups.

Users see the thumbnail and the title before they watch a video. The pair compresses what the video is about and what it feels like, and they are the first cues used to choose content. Yangandul et al. (2018), in an eye-tracking study of video browsing, reported that users looked at thumbnails before titles and gave thumbnails about twice as much visual attention. People, color, text, and scenes in the thumbnail, and the length, style, and vocabulary of the title, therefore need to be read as one multimodal unit.

These forms of presentation can vary by age. Older adults may have more trouble than younger adults reading low-contrast text (Warrington et al., 2018), and the purposes and habits of online video use also differ by age (Kim et al., 2023). Less is known about how such differences show up as habits of presentation in the thumbnails and titles of actual YouTube videos.

Age in this setting is more than a user attribute. If people, actions, objects, and wording tied to a given age keep recurring, a line can form between content associated with younger users and content associated with older users. Xu (2022) showed that public-authority social media images can place older adults in a narrow set of activities and settings that mark them off from other age groups. Analyzing age-related multimodal style is a way to see how age, as a social category, is written into digital space and made visible there. The analysis is not a claim about what a group prefers.

Computational work on thumbnails and multimodal social media has been growing. Papadamou et al. (2020) used titles, thumbnails, tags, and video content together to describe and detect YouTube videos that were inappropriate for children. Xue et al. (2026) looked at short-form videos recommended to children and teenagers at the feature level and the content level, and showed that unsafe content can be separated by scene and theme as well as by physical audiovisual features. Both studies concern children or adolescents and harmful content, so they do not tell us whether multimodal presentation also shifts by age in ordinary information videos that are not overtly harmful.

Thumbnail style has also been shown to sit inside a sociocultural setting. Limpijankit and Kender (2024) compared YouTube thumbnails from U.S. and Chinese news channels and found differences in color, brightness, composition, and shot scale. They also argued that image statistics follow scene content closely, so style should be compared inside similar visual themes. Their study, however, was built around two international news events and a country contrast. It did not take up age, and it did not treat the language of titles.

A few studies look at age and thumbnails more directly. Kim and Kim (2025) surveyed users of nail-related YouTube videos and reported age differences in preferred typography, character count, layout, and compositional elements. The sample was limited to one beauty genre and to self-reports from female users. There is still little work that takes thumbnails and titles together on large, in-the-wild platform data, or that compares content associated with people aged 34 and under and 65 and over across several categories.

Most prior comparisons stay inside one topic: harmful content, news, or beauty, for example. That is a good way to describe the local habit of a genre. It is a weak way to tell whether a difference belongs only to that topic or turns up again elsewhere. If the goal is age-related style, a gap found in one category should not be treated as a property of the age group. Signals that keep the same direction across categories need to be separated from signals that appear only in one topic.

Two gaps remain in the prior work. Few studies compare how the thumbnails and titles of ordinary information content relate to age across categories. Image and text have also usually been analyzed separately, so the style formed by the two modalities together has not been adequately described.

This paper analyzes thumbnail and title style associated with the 34-and-under and 65-and-over groups in four categories: EDU, HEALTH, LIFESTYLE, and SOCIETY. The aim is to separate cross-category signals, which are consistent in direction across all four categories, from category-specific signals, which appear in only one category or reverse direction. The comparison is intended to prevent a convention found in a single topic from being treated as the style of an age group, while still identifying age-related forms that recur across information domains.

The analysis does not begin with a predefined feature list. A fixed list can miss cues that were not anticipated. A model is therefore trained first to distinguish the two age groups from the thumbnail alone. The regions it uses are inspected, and only then are those regions operationalized as physical features and semantic units. A DINOv3 image classifier and Grad-CAM are used for this search. Grad-CAM is not taken as a conclusion about age-related style; it marks candidate regions for later tests.

These candidates are then examined at three layers. The first layer quantifies visual features: text area, text–background contrast, color, person area, facial expression, head direction, and estimated person age. The second converts scenes that a single feature cannot capture into LLaVA captions and compares people, actions, objects, affect, and setting with BERTopic and GEE. The third compares title length, orthography, grammar, and word origin, and examines lexical and contextual cues with KLUE-RoBERTa and LIME. The three layers connect where the model looks, what that region means, and how the difference appears in visual and linguistic features.

Two research questions follow.

- **RQ1.** How do the physical and semantic visual signals of thumbnails, and the formal and lexical properties of titles, differ between content associated with people aged 34 and under and content associated with people aged 65 and over?
- **RQ2.** Do these age-related multimodal signals appear across EDU, HEALTH, LIFESTYLE, and SOCIETY, or do they change with category context?

In thumbnails associated with the 65-and-over group, large text regions and strong text–background contrast appeared in all four categories. Grad-CAM and ROI analyses also identified person regions as discriminative cues in correctly classified 65-and-over cases, but differences in person area, expression, action, and background scenes varied by category. Titles associated with the 65-and-over group were longer on average and used vocabulary of life circumstances, illness, religion, and self-cultivation. Titles associated with the 34-and-under group used more loanwords and terms for content format and activity. Age-related style, on this evidence, combines conventions that cut across categories with conventions that remain genre-specific, rather than a single fixed grammar.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it treats age as a social category expressed in thumbnails and titles, not only as a user attribute. Second, it links Grad-CAM localization, measured visual features, VLM-based scene analysis, and the style and vocabulary of titles in one framework. Third, by comparing several content categories together, it distinguishes the cross-category consistency of age-related style from its category specificity. The study does not assume a universal grammar; it identifies presentation conventions that recur across domains.

These results do not demonstrate creators' targeting intent, users' actual preferences, or a split in information exposure by age. They show how presentation is associated with age before recommendation and exposure. This provides an empirical basis for discussing the representational boundaries that distinguish age groups in digital space, and for work on age-aware accessibility, multimodal content design, and platform media literacy.
