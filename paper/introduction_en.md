# 1. Introduction

YouTube has become a major digital space for news, health, education, and everyday information, beyond entertainment. Older adults also use online video for practical knowledge and lifelong learning. Through interviews and large-scale viewing logs from K-MOOC lecture videos, Kim et al. (2023) found that older adults learn material they can apply in daily life and that their viewing practices and difficulties differ from those of younger users. As different generations share the same platform, it has become important to understand how content is presented, in appearance and in language, across age groups.

Users encounter the thumbnail and the title before they watch a video. The two elements compress the content and tone of a video and serve as the first cues for selection. In an eye-tracking study of video browsing, Yangandul et al. (2018) found that users looked at thumbnails before titles and allocated about twice as much visual attention to thumbnails. People, color, text, and scenes in the thumbnail, together with the length, style, and vocabulary of the title, therefore need to be examined as a single multimodal unit.

These forms of presentation can vary by age. Older adults may have more difficulty than younger adults reading low-contrast text (Warrington et al., 2018), and the purposes and practices of online video use also differ by age (Kim et al., 2023). Less is known about how such differences appear as presentation conventions in the thumbnails and titles of actual YouTube videos.

Age in this study is first a channel-level audience label: content associated with viewers aged 34 and under, or with viewers aged 65 and over. That label is not treated as a theory of identity. The question is narrower. When people, actions, objects, and wording associated with one age group recur in thumbnails and titles, those elements can mark a difference between the two groups' content. Xu (2022) showed that public-authority social media images can place older adults in a restricted set of activities and settings. We ask whether ordinary information videos on YouTube likewise show measurable visual and textual conventions that distinguish content associated with younger audiences from content associated with older audiences. The analysis describes those conventions. It is not a claim about group preference or about creators' targeting intent.

Computational research on thumbnails and multimodal social media content has expanded steadily. Papadamou et al. (2020) combined YouTube titles, thumbnails, tags, and video content to characterize and detect videos that were inappropriate for children. Xue et al. (2026) analyzed short-form videos recommended to children and teenagers at the feature level and the content level, and showed that unsafe content can be distinguished by scene and theme as well as by physical audiovisual features. Both studies focus on children or adolescents and on harmful content, and therefore do not address whether multimodal presentation also varies by age in ordinary information videos that are not explicitly harmful.

Thumbnail style has also been shown to intersect with sociocultural context. Limpijankit and Kender (2024) compared YouTube thumbnails from U.S. and Chinese news channels and found differences in color, brightness, composition, and shot scale. They also argued that image statistics depend heavily on scene content, so style should be compared within similar visual themes. Their study, however, focused on two international news events and a cross-national comparison. It did not address age-related differences or the language of titles.

A smaller body of work examines age and thumbnails more directly. Kim and Kim (2025) surveyed users of nail-related YouTube videos and reported age differences in preferred typography, character count, layout, and compositional elements. The study was limited to one beauty genre and to self-reports from female users. Few studies jointly analyze thumbnails and titles in large-scale, in-the-wild platform data, or compare content associated with people aged 34 and under and 65 and over across several categories.

Most prior comparisons remain within a single topic, such as harmful content, news, or beauty. This approach is useful for describing the conventions of a given genre, but it is a weak basis for judging whether a difference is confined to that topic or recurs across domains. If the aim is to understand age-related style, a difference found in one category should not be treated as a property of the age group. Signals that are consistent in direction across categories need to be separated from signals that appear only in one topic.

Two gaps remain in the prior work. Few studies compare how the thumbnails and titles of ordinary information content relate to age across categories. Image and text have also usually been analyzed separately, so the style formed by the two modalities together has not been adequately described.

This paper analyzes thumbnail and title style associated with the 34-and-under and 65-and-over groups in four categories: EDU, HEALTH, LIFESTYLE, and SOCIETY. The samples are the Psychology education, Wellness, Meditation, and Current Affairs subcategories, respectively. The aim is to separate cross-category signals, which are consistent in direction across all four categories, from category-specific signals, which appear in only one category or reverse direction. The comparison is intended to prevent a convention found in a single topic from being treated as the style of an age group, while still identifying age-related forms that recur across information domains.

Thumbnails associated with the two age groups can be compared on familiar cues such as text, people, and color. Because a thumbnail layers these elements in one frame, the first task is to see which regions of the frame those cues should be read from. A DINOv3 image classifier and Grad-CAM are used to identify the regions the model attends to when it separates the two age groups, and those regions then set the focus for measuring physical features and semantic units.

These candidates are then examined at three layers. The first layer quantifies visual features: text area, text–background contrast, color, person area, facial expression, head direction, and estimated person age. The second converts scenes that a single feature cannot capture into LLaVA captions and compares people, actions, objects, affect, and setting with BERTopic and GEE. The third compares title length, orthography, grammar, and word origin, and examines lexical and contextual cues with KLUE-RoBERTa and LIME. The three layers connect where the model looks, what that region means, and how the difference appears in visual and linguistic features.

Two research questions follow.

- **RQ1.** How do the physical and semantic visual signals of thumbnails, and the formal and lexical properties of titles, differ between content associated with people aged 34 and under and content associated with people aged 65 and over?
- **RQ2.** Do these age-related multimodal signals appear across EDU, HEALTH, LIFESTYLE, and SOCIETY, or do they change with category context?

In thumbnails associated with the 65-and-over group, large text regions and strong text–background contrast appeared in all four categories. Grad-CAM and ROI analyses also identified person regions as discriminative cues in correctly classified 65-and-over cases, but differences in person area, expression, action, and background scenes varied by category. Titles associated with the 65-and-over group were longer on average and used vocabulary of life circumstances, illness, religion, and self-cultivation. Titles associated with the 34-and-under group used more loanwords and terms for content format and activity. Age-related style, on this evidence, combines conventions that cut across categories with conventions that remain genre-specific, rather than a single fixed grammar.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it shows that audience age is visible in the visual features of thumbnails and the textual features of titles, rather than remaining only a metadata label on channels. Second, it links Grad-CAM localization, measured visual features, VLM-based scene analysis, and the style and vocabulary of titles in one framework. Third, by comparing several content categories together, it distinguishes the cross-category consistency of age-related style from its category specificity. The study does not assume a universal grammar; it identifies presentation conventions that recur across domains.

These results do not demonstrate creators' targeting intent, users' actual preferences, or a split in information exposure by age. They show how presentation is associated with age before recommendation and exposure. This provides an empirical basis for describing those conventions, and for work on age-aware accessibility, visual and textual content design, and platform media literacy.
