# 1. Introduction

YouTube has become a major digital space for encountering information about news, health, education, and everyday life, beyond entertainment alone. Online video is also used by older adults as a medium for practical knowledge and lifelong learning. Drawing on interviews and large-scale usage logs, Kim et al. (2023) report that older adults learn everyday, applicable content through online video and exhibit viewing practices and difficulties that differ from those of younger users. As different generations share the same platform, it has become important to understand how content is presented—in appearance and in language—across age groups.

Users encounter thumbnails and titles before they watch a video. The two elements compress the content and tone of a video and serve as initial cues for selection. In an eye-tracking study, Yangandul et al. (2018) found that, during video browsing, users looked at thumbnails before titles and allocated about twice as much visual attention to thumbnails. This suggests that people, color, text, and scenes in the thumbnail, together with the length, style, and vocabulary of the title, should be examined as a single multimodal information unit.

Such modes of presentation may differ by age group. Older adults can experience greater visual difficulty than younger adults when reading low-contrast text (Warrington et al., 2018), and age-related differences are also observed in why and how people use online video (Kim et al., 2023). What remains insufficiently known is how these differences appear as expressive conventions in the thumbnails and titles of actual YouTube content.

Here, age is not merely a user attribute. When depictions of people, actions, objects, and language associated with a particular age are repeated, a representational boundary can form that distinguishes content associated with younger users from content associated with older users. Xu (2022) showed that, in social media images managed by public authorities, older adults may be portrayed in a restricted set of activities and contexts that set them apart from other age groups. Analyzing age-related multimodal style is therefore not an attempt to assert the preferences of a given group, but an effort to understand how age, as a social category, is expressed and made visible in digital space.

Computational analyses of thumbnails and multimodal social media content have accumulated steadily. Papadamou et al. (2020) combined YouTube titles, thumbnails, tags, and video content to characterize and detect inappropriate videos targeting children. Xue et al. (2026) analyzed short-form videos recommended to children and teenagers at both the feature level and the content level, showing that unsafe content can be distinguished not only by physical audiovisual features but also at the level of scenes and themes. Because both studies focus on children, adolescents, and harmful content, they do not explain whether multimodal expression also varies by age group in ordinary information content that is not explicitly harmful.

Visual thumbnail style has also been shown to intersect with sociocultural context. Comparing YouTube thumbnails from U.S. and Chinese news channels, Limpijankit and Kender (2024) found that color, brightness, composition, and shot scale can differ by cultural background. They further noted that statistical properties of images depend heavily on scene content, and that style should therefore be compared within similar visual themes. That study, however, focused on two international news events and a cross-national comparison; it did not address age-related differences or the linguistic expression of titles.

A smaller body of work examines thumbnail differences by age more directly. In a survey of users of nail-related YouTube content, Kim and Kim (2025) reported that preferences for typography, character count, layout, and compositional elements can vary with age. That study, however, focused on a specific beauty genre and on the self-reported preferences of female users. Studies that jointly analyze thumbnails and titles in large-scale, in-the-wild platform content, or that compare content associated with people aged 34 and under and 65 and over across multiple categories, remain limited.

Prior work has often compared group differences within a single topic, such as harmful content, news, or beauty. Such designs are useful for describing the expressive conventions of a given content domain, but they make it difficult to judge whether an observed difference is confined to that topic or recurs across domains. Understanding age-related style requires distinguishing signals that run in the same direction across categories from signals that appear only in a particular topic, rather than generalizing a difference found in one category to an age group as a whole.

Taken together, two gaps remain. First, few studies compare how thumbnails and titles of ordinary information content are associated with age across multiple categories. Second, image and text have typically been analyzed separately, so the age-related style that the two modalities jointly compose has not been adequately described.

To address these gaps, we analyze the multimodal style of thumbnails and titles associated with the 34-and-under and 65-and-over groups in four categories: EDU, HEALTH, LIFESTYLE, and SOCIETY. The aim is to separate cross-category signals that run in the same direction across all four categories from category-specific signals that appear only in a given category or reverse direction. This allows us to identify age-related expressions that recur across information domains without overgeneralizing the conventions of a single topic to an age group as a whole.

The analysis begins with an exploratory stage so that the scope is not restricted to predefined features. Starting from a fixed feature set can miss unexpected cues; we therefore first inspect the regions attended to by a model and then instantiate those regions as physical features and semantic units. We first use a DINOv3-based image classifier and Grad-CAM to explore which thumbnail regions the model uses as evidence for age classification. Grad-CAM is used not to deliver conclusions about age-related style, but to locate candidate regions to be tested in subsequent analyses.

We then instantiate the exploratory findings at three layers. First, we quantify visual features such as text area, text–background contrast, color, the proportion of the frame occupied by people, facial expression, head orientation, and estimated person age. Second, we convert scenes that are difficult to describe with a single feature into LLaVA-based image captions and analyze differences in people, actions, objects, affect, and setting with BERTopic and GEE. Third, we compare title length, orthography, grammar, and word origin, and inspect lexical and contextual signals with KLUE-RoBERTa and LIME. This design connects where the model looks, what those regions mean, and how differences are realized as visual and linguistic features.

On this basis we pose two research questions:

- **RQ1.** How do the physical and semantic visual signals of thumbnails, and the formal and lexical properties of titles, differ between content associated with people aged 34 and under and content associated with people aged 65 and over?
- **RQ2.** Do these age-related multimodal signals appear in common across EDU, HEALTH, LIFESTYLE, and SOCIETY, or do they take different forms depending on category context?

The analyses show that, in thumbnails associated with the 65-and-over group, large text regions and strong text–background contrast appear consistently across categories. Grad-CAM and ROI analyses also identify person regions as discriminative cues in correctly classified 65-and-over cases; however, group differences in person proportion, expression, action, and background scenes reverse or vary by category. In titles, the 65-and-over group is associated with relatively longer titles and with vocabulary of life circumstances, illness, religion, and self-cultivation, whereas the 34-and-under group is associated with loanwords and with vocabulary of content format and activity. Age-related style is thus not a single fixed grammar, but a structure in which conventions that cut across categories combine with genre-specific context.

This paper makes three contributions. First, it treats age not as a mere user attribute but as a social category expressed visually and linguistically through thumbnails and titles. Second, it offers a multilayered analytical framework that links Grad-CAM localization, explicit visual-feature analysis, VLM-based scene analysis, and stylistic and lexical analysis of titles. Third, by analyzing multiple content categories together rather than a single topic, it distinguishes the cross-category consistency of age-related style from its category specificity. In this way, the study identifies expressive conventions that recur across domains without presupposing a universal grammar.

These results do not directly demonstrate creators’ targeting intent, users’ actual preferences, or a separation of information exposure by age. They do, however, show how modes of presentation are associated with age at the stage before recommendation and exposure, and thereby provide empirical grounds for understanding the representational boundaries that distinguish age groups in digital space. They can also serve as a basis for discussing age-aware information accessibility, multimodal content design, and platform media literacy.
