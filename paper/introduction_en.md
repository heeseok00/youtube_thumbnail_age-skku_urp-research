# 1. Introduction

YouTube is now a main digital space for news, health, education, and everyday information, not only for entertainment. Older adults also use online video for practical knowledge and lifelong learning. In interviews and large-scale usage logs, Kim et al. (2023) found that older adults learn material they can apply in daily life and that their viewing practices and difficulties differ from those of younger users. Once several generations share one platform, it matters how content is shown and worded for different age groups.

Users see the thumbnail and the title before they watch a video. The pair compresses what the video is about and what it feels like, and they are the first cues used to choose content. Yangandul et al. (2018), in an eye-tracking study of video browsing, reported that users looked at thumbnails before titles and gave thumbnails about twice as much visual attention. People, color, text, and scenes in the thumbnail, and the length, style, and vocabulary of the title, therefore need to be read as one multimodal unit.

These forms of presentation can vary by age. Older adults may have more trouble than younger adults reading low-contrast text (Warrington et al., 2018), and the purposes and habits of online video use also differ by age (Kim et al., 2023). Less is known about how such differences show up as habits of presentation in the thumbnails and titles of actual YouTube videos.

Age in this setting is more than a user attribute. If people, actions, objects, and wording tied to a given age keep recurring, a line can form between content associated with younger users and content associated with older users. Xu (2022) showed that public-authority social media images can place older adults in a narrow set of activities and settings that mark them off from other age groups. Analyzing age-related multimodal style is a way to see how age, as a social category, is written into digital space and made visible there. The analysis is not a claim about what a group prefers.

Computational work on thumbnails and multimodal social media has been growing. Papadamou et al. (2020) used titles, thumbnails, tags, and video content together to describe and detect YouTube videos that were inappropriate for children. Xue et al. (2026) looked at short-form videos recommended to children and teenagers at the feature level and the content level, and showed that unsafe content can be separated by scene and theme as well as by physical audiovisual features. Both studies concern children or adolescents and harmful content, so they do not tell us whether multimodal presentation also shifts by age in ordinary information videos that are not overtly harmful.

Thumbnail style has also been shown to sit inside a sociocultural setting. Limpijankit and Kender (2024) compared YouTube thumbnails from U.S. and Chinese news channels and found differences in color, brightness, composition, and shot scale. They also argued that image statistics follow scene content closely, so style should be compared inside similar visual themes. Their study, however, was built around two international news events and a country contrast. It did not take up age, and it did not treat the language of titles.

A few studies look at age and thumbnails more directly. Kim and Kim (2025) surveyed users of nail-related YouTube videos and reported age differences in preferred typography, character count, layout, and compositional elements. The sample was limited to one beauty genre and to self-reports from female users. There is still little work that takes thumbnails and titles together on large, in-the-wild platform data, or that compares content associated with people aged 34 and under and 65 and over across several categories.

Most prior comparisons stay inside one topic: harmful content, news, or beauty, for example. That is a good way to describe the local habit of a genre. It is a weak way to tell whether a difference belongs only to that topic or turns up again elsewhere. If the goal is age-related style, a gap found in one category should not be treated as a property of the age group. Signals that keep the same direction across categories need to be separated from signals that appear only in one topic.

Two gaps follow from this literature. Few studies compare how the thumbnails and titles of ordinary information content relate to age across categories. Image and text have also usually been analyzed on their own, so the style that the two modalities form together has been left thin.

This paper analyzes thumbnail and title style associated with the 34-and-under and 65-and-over groups in four categories: EDU, HEALTH, LIFESTYLE, and SOCIETY. The aim is to split cross-category signals, which hold the same direction in all four categories, from category-specific signals, which appear in only one category or change direction. The comparison is meant to keep a single topic's habit from being read as the style of an age group, while still showing which age-related forms repeat across information domains.

The analysis does not start from a feature list fixed in advance. A preset list can hide cues the researcher did not already have in mind. A model is therefore trained first to separate the two age groups from the thumbnail alone; the regions it uses are inspected, and only then are those regions turned into physical features and semantic units. A DINOv3 image classifier and Grad-CAM are used for this search. Grad-CAM is not treated as a verdict on age-related style. It is used to mark candidate regions that later analyses have to test.

Those candidates are then specified at three layers. The first layer quantifies visual features: text area, text–background contrast, color, person area, facial expression, head direction, and estimated person age. The second converts scenes that a single feature cannot carry into LLaVA captions and compares people, actions, objects, affect, and setting with BERTopic and GEE. The third compares title length, spelling conventions, grammar, and word origin, and reads lexical and contextual cues with KLUE-RoBERTa and LIME. The three layers are meant to link where the model looks, what that region means, and how the difference is realized in visual and linguistic features.

Two research questions follow.

- **RQ1.** How do the physical and semantic visual signals of thumbnails, and the formal and lexical properties of titles, differ between content associated with people aged 34 and under and content associated with people aged 65 and over?
- **RQ2.** Do these age-related multimodal signals appear across EDU, HEALTH, LIFESTYLE, and SOCIETY, or do they change with category context?

In thumbnails associated with the 65-and-over group, large text regions and strong text–background contrast appeared in all four categories. Grad-CAM and ROI analyses also marked person regions as cues in correctly classified 65-and-over cases, but differences in person area, expression, action, and background scenes did not keep one direction across categories. Titles associated with the 65-and-over group were longer on average and drew on vocabulary of life circumstances, illness, religion, and self-cultivation. Titles associated with the 34-and-under group leaned on loanwords and on terms for format and activity. On this evidence, age-related style mixes habits that cross categories with habits that stay inside a genre, rather than following one fixed grammar.

The paper has three contributions. It treats age as a social category that is shown in thumbnails and titles, rather than as a user attribute alone. It connects Grad-CAM localization, measured visual features, VLM-based scene analysis, and title style and vocabulary in one framework. It also compares several content categories at once, so that the cross-category consistency of age-related style can be told apart from its category specificity. A universal grammar is not assumed; the claim is only that some habits of presentation repeat across domains.

None of this proves that creators target age groups, that users prefer these forms, or that information exposure is split by age. What the results show is how presentation relates to age before recommendation and exposure. That is enough to ground a discussion of the lines that mark age groups in digital space, and of age-aware accessibility, multimodal content design, and platform media literacy.
