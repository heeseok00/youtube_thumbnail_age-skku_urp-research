# 1. Introduction

YouTube has become a main route to news, health, education, and everyday information. This informational content belongs to no single generation. Older adults also use online video for practical knowledge and lifelong learning, and Kim et al. (2023), drawing on interviews and large-scale viewing logs from K-MOOC lecture videos, reported that they learn material they can apply in daily life while showing viewing practices and difficulties that differ from those of younger users. Different generations therefore rely on the same platform for information.

Watching begins with a choice made before the video is opened. Users encounter the thumbnail and the title first. The two elements condense the content and tone of a video and provide the initial cues for selection. In an eye-tracking study of video browsing, Yangandul et al. (2018) found that users looked at thumbnails before titles and allocated about twice as much visual attention to thumbnails. The people, colors, text, and scenes in a thumbnail, together with the length, style, and vocabulary of its title, therefore need to be examined as a paired multimodal presentation unit.

This first screen works differently for different viewers. Older adults may experience greater difficulty than younger adults when reading low-contrast text (Warrington et al., 2018). How such differences appear as presentation conventions in the thumbnails and titles of actual YouTube content remains largely unknown.

Age in this study is a channel-level audience label, marking content associated with viewers aged 34 and under or with viewers aged 65 and over. The label opens a narrow and measurable question. When people, actions, objects, and wording associated with one age group recur in thumbnails and titles, those elements work as conventions that distinguish the two groups' content. Xu (2022) showed that social media images managed by public authorities can place older adults in a restricted set of activities and settings. We ask whether ordinary information videos on YouTube likewise carry measurable visual and textual conventions that separate content associated with younger audiences from content associated with older audiences, and we describe those conventions as they appear at the moment of selection.

**Related Work**

Computational research on thumbnails and multimodal social media content has expanded steadily. Papadamou et al. (2020) combined YouTube titles, thumbnails, tags, and video content to characterize and detect videos that were inappropriate for children. Xue et al. (2026) analyzed short-form videos recommended to children and teenagers at the feature level and the content level, and showed that unsafe content can be distinguished by scene and theme as well as by physical audiovisual features. Both studies focus on children or adolescents and on harmful content, and therefore leave open whether multimodal presentation also varies by age in ordinary information videos that carry no explicit harm.

Thumbnail style has also been shown to intersect with sociocultural context. Limpijankit and Kender (2024) compared YouTube thumbnails from U.S. and Chinese news channels and found differences in color, brightness, composition, and shot scale. They also argued that image statistics depend heavily on scene content, so style should be compared within similar visual themes. Their study, however, focused on two international news events and a cross-national comparison, leaving age-related differences and the language of titles outside its scope.

A few studies look at age and thumbnails more directly. Kim and Kim (2025) surveyed users of nail-related YouTube videos and reported age differences in preferred typography, character count, layout, and compositional elements. The sample was limited to one beauty genre and to self-reports from female users. Work that takes thumbnails and titles together on large, in-the-wild platform data, or that compares content associated with people aged 34 and under and 65 and over across several categories, remains scarce.

Most prior comparisons remain within a single topic, such as harmful content, news, or beauty. This approach describes the conventions of a given genre well, but it leaves open whether an observed difference is confined to that topic or recurs across domains. Understanding age-related style therefore requires separating signals that hold their direction across categories from signals that appear in only one topic.

**Research Gaps**

Two gaps remain in the prior work. Few studies compare how the thumbnails and titles of ordinary information content relate to age across categories. Image and text have also usually been analyzed separately, so the style formed by the two modalities together remains underdescribed.

This paper analyzes the multimodal style of thumbnails and titles associated with the 34-and-under and 65-and-over groups in four categories: EDU, HEALTH, LIFESTYLE, and SOCIETY, sampled from the subcategories Psychology Education, Wellness, Meditation, and Current Affairs. The aim is to separate cross-category signals, which hold the same direction in all four categories, from category-specific signals, which appear in only one category or reverse direction. Reading the four categories within one framework identifies the age-related forms that recur across information domains while keeping the conventions of a single topic distinct from them.

Thumbnails associated with the two groups can be compared through familiar cues such as text, people, and color. Because these elements overlap within one frame, the same feature yields different values depending on the region it is read from. A DINOv3 classifier is therefore trained to separate the 34-and-under and 65-and-over groups from the thumbnail alone, and Grad-CAM shows which regions the model relies on. Those regions set the focus for the feature measurements that follow.

The exploration is then made concrete at three layers. The first layer quantifies visual features: text area, text–background contrast, color, person area, facial expression, head direction, and estimated person age. The second converts scenes that a single feature cannot capture into LLaVA captions and compares people, actions, objects, affect, and setting with BERTopic and GEE. The third compares title length, orthography, grammar, and word origin, and examines lexical and contextual cues with KLUE-RoBERTa and LIME. The three layers connect where the model looks, what that region means, and how the difference is realized in visual and linguistic features.

Two research questions follow.

- **RQ1.** How do the physical and semantic visual signals of thumbnails, and the formal and lexical properties of titles, differ between content associated with people aged 34 and under and content associated with people aged 65 and over?
- **RQ2.** Do these age-related multimodal signals appear across EDU, HEALTH, LIFESTYLE, and SOCIETY, or do they change with category context?

In thumbnails associated with the 65-and-over group, large text regions and strong text–background contrast appeared in all four categories. Grad-CAM and ROI analyses also identified person regions as discriminative cues in correctly classified 65-and-over cases, while differences in person area, expression, action, and background scenes varied by category. Titles associated with the 65-and-over group were longer on average and used vocabulary of life circumstances, illness, religion, and self-cultivation. Titles associated with the 34-and-under group used more loanwords and terms for content format and activity. Age-related style, on this evidence, combines conventions that cut across categories with conventions that remain genre-specific, rather than a single fixed grammar.

This paper makes three contributions. First, it offers a data-driven comparison of age-related visual and textual signals in YouTube thumbnails and titles across four information categories, providing one of the first large-scale accounts of how informational content associated with the 34-and-under and 65-and-over audiences is presented. Second, by comparing these categories within one framework, it separates signals that recur across categories from those specific to a given category, distinguishing age-related style from single-domain convention. Third, by analyzing thumbnails and titles jointly, linking Grad-CAM localization, visual features, VLM-based scene semantics, and title style and vocabulary, it shows how age-related expression is composed across modalities rather than in either alone.

These results show how presentation is associated with audience age before recommendation and exposure, at the point where a viewer decides what to open. They provide an empirical basis for describing these conventions, and for work on age-aware accessibility, multimodal content design, and platform media literacy.
